## 1. FinTech / InsurTech

**By Income Insurance Limited**

> **How might we reimagine insurance, powered by data and AI, so that climate risk protection becomes affordable, accessible, and actionable for every vulnerable household and SME in Singapore?**

### Why does this matter now?

Singapore is getting hotter and wetter, and in El Niño years drier and hazier. Days above 35°C, about 21 a year, could reach 41 to 351 a year by 2100 depending on emissions; extreme rainfall is rising in every season; and this year's haze, the longest since 2015, is already estimated to cost more than the 2015 episode. From 1 December 2026, four further heat-stress measures became mandatory for outdoor work. Open real-time weather and air-quality data and AI now make it cheaper to price these risks, pay out automatically, and reach customers once too costly to serve.

### Context and Background

**Current state:** How is this problem handled today, and why does that fall short?

- **Households.** Flood damage to a home's contents and renovations can be covered by comprehensive home insurance, but this is voluntary.
- **Workers.** About 71,600 residents were regular platform workers (taxi, private-hire or delivery) in 2025. When haze, extreme heat or heavy rain makes outdoor work unsafe, they and other outdoor workers such as those in the construction industry could lose earnings. Work injury compensation covers injury, not the income lost by stopping work as a precaution.
- **SMEs.** Similar to workers, SMEs can be impacted by business interruptions from extreme weather events as well. However, most insurance products cover property / asset damage and no business interruptions. However, it may also be difficult to assess business interruptions without physical damage.
- **Increased frequency and mass impact.** Extreme weather events like floods, typhoons, heatwaves and haze can impact an entire nation or region concurrently. Traditional insurance provide high coverage with relatively expensive premiums, which can overwhelm insurers in terms of capital requirements during mass extreme weather events, making such events uninsurable. In addition, traditional insurance typically requires loss assessments which are slow and expensive to operate and which can overwhelm an insurer in terms of operations during extreme weather events. The long claims process also prevents liquidity from reaching claimants fast, which is important for disaster recovery.

**What has been tried:** Approaches that have been tried, and where they stalled.

- In 2018, Income Insurance launched Droplet, a parametric-based insurance, that offers a pay-out when prices on ride-hailing platforms surge due to rain. When covered by Droplet, consumers can expect a pay-out of up to 60% of their trip fare and/or cancellation fee when they hail a ride in Singapore on a ride-hailing platform if it was raining at the point of pick-up. For a premium of no more than $9.60 a day, consumers can submit as many ride receipts for claim, up to a maximum of $50 on the same day.
- Traditional commercial insurance from Income covers property damage and business interruption losses that are related to property damage.
- Traditional travel insurance covers most natural disasters and weather-related events but requires some form of evidence of travel disruption (e.g. trips or flights cancelled).

**In Singapore today**

- Parametric products sold in Singapore includes Singlife's rainfall-triggered travel cover (2022, up to S$150), Swiss Re Corporate Solutions' PSI-triggered haze cover for businesses (2018) and flight-delay covers. None reaches households, workers or small traders.

**Overseas, heat and lost income**

- **India, SEWA (2023–2026):** heat-index income cover for women informal workers grew from 21,000 to about 250,000 members, with ICICI Lombard as insurer and Swiss Re capacity. 2023 paid nothing because thresholds were set at record heat; 2024 paid 93% of members (₹151–1,656 each) after thresholds were reset to local history; 2025 paid very little. Payouts took six to eight weeks and premiums remain part-paid by donors.
- **India, Delhi gig workers (2025 trial):** ₹200 paid automatically on the first forecast day of a heatwave cut lost workdays by about two-thirds, but workers' willingness to pre-pay for the same cover was low.
- **China:** ZhongAn's 37°C cover (2014) sold over 200,000 policies at ¥10 but paid almost nothing in a cool summer; its single threshold ignored humidity and the regulator warned against gambling-like products. Meituan (2025) pays the premium for heat-illness cover for over 3 million riders, issued with each order, but it pays on diagnosis rather than temperature, and its heat allowances require riders to complete more orders in the heat.
- **Japan (2022–):** heatstroke cover from ¥100 a day in the PayPay app has passed 400,000 policies, mostly bought by families for children and elderly parents; it is a medical product, not income protection.
- **Hong Kong, AXA (2024):** employer-paid cover at HK$19.9 a head pays HK$100 for a cooling kit `[rebuilt]` when temperature exceeds 36.0°C for three consecutive days; uptake not published.
- **South Korea (2025–2026):** Jeju province pays for cover that gives day labourers about KRW 17,000 an hour, up to four hours, when a heat warning halts work on public sites. KB Insurance's cover for market stallholders (heat above 33°C pays KRW 15,000 a day; rain above 80 mm up to KRW 50,000 a day) had no subscribers ten months after launch because nobody would carry the premium.

**Overseas, rain and flood**

- **Indonesia, Igloo × DANA (Sep 2026):** IDR 500 (about S$0.04) at checkout buys an automatic IDR 50,000 payout if satellite rainfall at the buyer's location reaches 2.5 mm that day; no hardware. It is three weeks old, so untested against people buying only when rain is forecast.
- **Jakarta flood card (2009–2010):** IDR 50,000 premium, IDR 250,000 payout when the Manggarai gate reached 950 cm; about 50 sold; residents did not see one city gauge as their own risk; discontinued.
- **Caribbean Livelihood Protection Policy (2013–, relaunched Dec 2025):** wind and rain index for vendors, fishers and taxi drivers; US$48 a year for US$370 of cover `[rebuilt]`, a 13% rate that proved unaffordable for the lowest-income, and demand faded in years without payouts.
- **Guatemala, MiCRO (2017–):** rain and earthquake cover bundled with micro-loans at 5% of the loan; 70–80% renewal; the claims ratio reached 100% in 2018 and triggers had to be tightened. Its Haiti predecessor exhausted its limit after Hurricane Sandy and stopped.
- **Nairobi, BlockBima (2025):** rain above a threshold at the trader's own market automatically reduced loan repayments for 203 market traders, settled in one day; 86% preferred the insured loan.
- **UK, FloodFlash (2019–):** parametric flood cover for small businesses paid within hours, but it depends on a sensor installed at each premises, which this brief rules out as the core of a solution.

**Constraints that solutions must respect:** For example regulation, cost ceilings, literacy or language, connectivity, existing systems.

- **Licensed insurer and fair dealing:** products must be offered by an MAS-licensed insurer and meet MAS fair-dealing and disclosure rules. Design with Income, not as a new insurer.
- **Insurable interest:** sell only to people who would actually suffer a loss when the event happens, and size the payout taking into consideration the expected loss. A payout to someone with nothing to lose is a bet, not insurance. Singapore has no specific rule on parametric payouts, so assume insurable interest must be shown at the point of sale.
- **Say plainly when it does not pay:** one sentence at the point of sale that the product pays on the trigger, and that a customer can suffer a loss and receive nothing. Several trigger levels make the no-pay level obvious.
- **Back-test the trigger:** show, from past NEA and PUB data, how often target customers would have lost income or property with no payout. Schemes whose first season paid nothing lost the customers they were built for.
- **Use the index people already act on:** NEA's heat-stress (WBGT) advisory levels, the 24-hour PSI bands, PUB's flood sensors and MSS rain gauges. The source must be independent, published, timely, with decades of history and a fallback if the feed fails. Worksite temperatures run higher than station readings; say how that gap is handled.
- **Decide who pays before designing:** member, platform, employer, union, or a time-limited start-up subsidy shown on the premium line with an end date. No permanent subsidy.
- **Pay based on needs:** Payouts should be made when claimants need them the most.
- **Cap frequency and pool perils:** cap the number of payable days per season, bundle heat, rain and haze, and budget for reinsurance, because haze hits every customer at once.
- **Affordability:** a few dollars a week or month. The Philippines caps micro-premiums at 7.5% of the daily minimum wage per day; Malaysia's Perlindungan Tenang covers cost RM50–75 a year; the Asia-Pacific average microinsurance premium is about US$13 a year; expenses should sit within about 20% of premium.
- **Understanding:** the trigger, the payout and the exclusions must fit in one SMS in the customer's language, and customers should be able to say back what the product does.
- **Data and AI:** PDPA (consent; reasonable, notified purposes); AI pricing must be explainable and must not price out the groups that most need cover (MAS FEAT principles).
- **Complement statutory cover:** platform workers have work-injury compensation from 1 Jan 2025 and MOM's heat-stress rules set rest breaks; the product should cover lost income from weather and haze, not duplicate injury cover.

**What was tried (full text):**

The SME SmartBiz 360 offers all-risk coverage for SMEs' properties and business assets if during the period of insurance, the insured property is physically lost, destroyed or damaged while contained in the premises. This includes business interruption up to a maximum of 100 days per policy year, for the period during which the business is interrupted or interfered with because of property loss or damage.

The product's key features include:

- Direct loss, damage, corruption, impairment, encryption or deletion of IT assets directly caused by malware, rendering all or part of IT assets unusable during the period of insurance for the conduct of business, SmartBiz 360 indemnifies the SME for the reasonable and necessary costs incurred to repair or replace IT assets which have been directly damaged or rendered permanently unusable by such malware; and reinstall, recover, restore or reconfigure operating systems, software applications, databases, programs, configurations, and digital records from available backups or other available records, to restore IT assets to substantially the same condition existing immediately prior to the loss.

[^1]: Accident or accidental means a sudden, unexpected event which happens during the period of insurance which must be the only cause of property damage affecting the business.

## 2. Sustainability / Climate Action

**By the Ministry of Energy, Trade and Industry**

> **How might we use technology to help smallholder farmers improve produce quality and consistency, and supply reliability, so that they can meet larger buyers' requirements and raise their incomes?**

### Why does this matter now?

*What has changed recently that makes this problem urgent or newly solvable?*

Agriculture remains central to India's economy, supporting employment, domestic food security and contributing approximately 12% of India's exports. However, Agriculture is increasingly affected by unpredictable weather and extreme climate events, which disrupt production volumes and product quality. Farmers with limited access to technology and infrastructure are less able to anticipate and manage these disruptions. Inconsistent supply prevents them from meeting larger buyers' requirements, while uncertain demand discourages investment in better production systems.

Recent efforts to modernize Indian agriculture present an opportunity to use technology to improve product consistency, reduce post-harvest losses, strengthen supply resilience and expand market access.

### Context and Background

**Current state:** How is this problem handled today, and why does that fall short?

Smallholder farmers have limited access to forecasting, production and supply-chain capabilities to manage weather-related disruptions. This affects the quality and quantity of their produce, making it difficult to meet larger buyers' requirements consistently, limiting their market access and income opportunities.

**What has been tried:** Approaches that have been tried, and where they stalled.

The Government of India has introduced initiatives to improve agricultural productivity, infrastructure and market access. Building on these efforts, technology can help smallholder farmers manage weather risks and strengthen coordination across the supply chain. Greater clarity on demand can support farmers' investment in production improvements, while more reliable supply volumes and consistent product quality can give buyers greater confidence to commit to purchase agreements.

Digital farm advisory and market-linkage platforms have been deployed in India, with varied commercial and adoption outcomes. Some private platforms have ceased operations amid funding and profitability challenges, while digital agricultural trading initiatives have demonstrated the importance of trusted quality assessment and supporting logistics. Hence, technology solutions should combine clear benefits for farmers and buyers with practical delivery arrangements and a sustainable operating model.

**Constraints that solutions must respect:** For example regulation, cost ceilings, literacy or language, connectivity, existing systems.

Solutions must be affordable, scalable and accessible to farmers with varying resources, digital capabilities and connectivity. Solutions should also consider the entire supply chain, including quality assessment, processing, storage and transportation, as each stage affects product quality and consistency.

## 3. Emerging Technologies

**By Singtel**

> **How might telcos use AI and emerging technologies to help enterprise sales teams better understand their customers, identify their needs and opportunities, and recommend the right next action — enabling them to deliver a more relevant customer experience and unlock new growth?**

### Why does this matter now?

Enterprise telcos compete in an increasingly global market, where sales teams must understand customers and opportunities in both their established markets and unfamiliar overseas markets where they have lesser presence and relationships. Yet customer intelligence is often fragmented across internal systems, market reports and external sources, making it difficult to identify prospects, anticipate needs and engage decision-makers effectively. With advances in AI, telcos can now uncover market insights, identify buying signals and recommend timely actions — enabling sales teams to hunt for new business more effectively, deepen existing relationships and compete beyond their home markets.

### Context and Background

**Current state:** How is this problem handled today, and why does that fall short?

Enterprise sales teams typically rely on CRM systems, customer interactions, internal account knowledge, market research, industry news and professional networks to identify prospects and sales opportunities. Much of this research is manual, time-consuming and dependent on individual experience and relationships. The challenge is greater in overseas markets, where teams may lack local market knowledge, established networks and visibility into potential customers.

Sales engagement also tends to be reactive, focusing on responding to individual customer requirements rather than anticipating evolving business needs. With information fragmented across multiple sources, opportunities can be missed to proactively engage customers, recommend relevant solutions and move beyond transactional selling towards strategic partnerships that co-create long-term business value.

**What has been tried:** Approaches that have been tried, and where they stalled.

Telcos have traditionally invested in CRM platforms, sales analytics, market intelligence tools and account management processes to better understand customers and identify business opportunities. More recently, AI-powered tools have emerged to automate research, analyse customer information, predict sales opportunities and recommend next actions.

However, these approaches often address individual parts of the sales journey rather than providing a holistic understanding of the customer. Information remains fragmented, insights may lack sufficient business context, and recommendations do not always translate into meaningful sales actions. These challenges become more pronounced when pursuing opportunities in unfamiliar international markets, where customer intelligence and established relationships may be limited.

The opportunity is to move beyond isolated tools and reactive selling towards more proactive, intelligence-driven engagement — helping telcos anticipate customer needs, discover untapped opportunities and co-create long-term business value.

**Constraints that solutions must respect:** For example regulation, cost ceilings, literacy or language, connectivity, existing systems.

Solutions should demonstrate clear business value, practical feasibility and the potential to drive sustainable revenue growth or stronger customer relationships.

Proposed ideas should consider real-world enterprise environments, including data privacy and security, integration with existing systems, and applicability across different markets, customer segments and industries.

While students are encouraged to challenge conventional approaches and explore emerging technologies, solutions should be scalable, commercially viable and capable of translating insights into meaningful actions for sales teams and customers.

### Why does this matter now?

Both societies are aging faster than their support systems can adapt. Singapore became a super-aged society in 2026, with 21.4% of citizens aged 65 and above, and this is projected to pass one in four by 2035.

### Context and Background

**Current state:** How is this problem handled today, and why does that fall short?

Ageing well has shifted from extending lifespan to extending healthspan, the years lived in good physical, mental and social health. It must now go further, to a holistic quality of life that includes connection, purpose and the chance to contribute. Rising smartphone use among seniors, low-cost digital tools and community programmes can make this affordable and scalable.

**What has been tried:** Approaches that have been tried, and where they stalled.

Singapore has built a national network of Active Ageing Centres, with a government target of 220 by end-2025, yet participation is uneven. Men can make up as little as 20% of participants at some centres, and programmes remain largely recreational rather than purpose-driven. Most also depend on paid staff and volunteers to run, which makes them manpower-intensive and costly to scale.
